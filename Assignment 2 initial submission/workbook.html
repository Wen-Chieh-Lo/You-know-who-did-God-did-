Twitch New-Channel Recommendation¶

CSE 158 / 258 — Assignment 2¶

Team: Cheng-Yan Juang, Wen-Chieh Lo & Cheng-Yi Chen
Repository: Wen-Chieh-Lo/You-know-who-did-God-did-

Research question. Can a viewer's past Twitch watch history predict 10 channels they have never watched before, and does combining ItemKNN with Bayesian Personalized Ranking improve ranking accuracy without collapsing recommendation diversity?

This workbook is designed to be readable without rerunning model training. It contains the final experiment outputs, the key modeling logic, the evaluation protocol, and the interpretation needed for the presentation. The full reproducible training pipeline remains in the GitHub repository.

Main takeaway¶

The validation-selected Hybrid (0.75 BPR + 0.25 ItemKNN after per-user score standardization) is the strongest ranking model on the held-out test. Its daily-case NDCG@10 is 0.13148, compared with 0.12584 for ItemKNN and 0.03779 for the recent-popularity baseline. The improvement over ItemKNN is modest but statistically significant under user-clustered paired bootstrap. The tradeoff is lower catalog coverage and novelty than ItemKNN.

1. Predictive Task¶

1.1 Problem formulation¶

We formulate the project as a Top-10 implicit-feedback ranking task.

For each known viewer, the system must rank channels that:

  1. existed in the model's fit-period catalog, and
  2. were not previously watched by that viewer.

A target is a channel that the viewer watches for the first time during the future evaluation window. Repeated viewing of a previously watched channel is intentionally not a positive for this task.

This makes the problem different from rating prediction or binary classification. We care about the relative order of many candidate channels, not merely whether one user-item pair is positive.

1.2 Leakage-safe temporal split¶

The source uses relative 10-minute time slots, not calendar timestamps. We convert them into relative 24-hour blocks and split entire days chronologically:

  • Train: days 0–29
  • Validation: days 30–35
  • Test: days 36–42

Hyperparameters are selected on Validation using a Train-only fit. Final models are then refit on Train + Validation and evaluated once on Test.

This is preferable to a random interaction split because a deployed recommender only has access to the past when predicting future behavior.

1.3 Evaluation goals and baselines¶

We use NDCG@10 as the main selection metric because a correct unseen channel is more useful when it appears near the top of the recommendation list. We also report:

  • Recall@10 — fraction of true future unseen channels recovered in the Top-10.
  • Hit Rate@10 — whether at least one future unseen channel is hit.
  • Catalog Coverage — fraction of the fit catalog that appears in recommendations.
  • Novelty (bits) — self-information under fit-period channel popularity.
  • Long-tail Exposure — fraction of recommendations from the low-popularity tail.
  • Candidate Coverage — fraction of future positives that even exist in the fit catalog.

The required simple baselines are Popularity and Recent Popularity (last 7 fit days). They are important because a personalized model is only useful if it meaningfully improves over a cheap non-personalized ranking.

The audit found no missing cells, exact duplicate rows, invalid durations, non-positive user IDs, or blank channel names. The dominant data challenge is therefore sparsity and cold start, not malformed input.

Pre-processing choices¶

  • Convert relative 10-minute slots into relative day indices.
  • Use a binary user-channel matrix as the main representation: watched = 1.
  • Keep only mappings learned from the fit period.
  • Exclude already-watched channels from each user's recommendation candidates.
  • Treat an unobserved channel as unknown, not as an explicit dislike.
  • Build labels only from first-time future user-channel interactions.

3. Modeling¶

We compare models that are appropriate for implicit feedback and ranking.

3.1 Popularity baselines¶

  • Popularity: channels ranked by number of distinct viewers in the fit period.
  • Recent Popularity: same idea, restricted to the most recent 7 fit days.

Advantages: extremely cheap, transparent, strong sanity check.
Disadvantages: non-personalized and strongly popularity-biased.

3.2 ItemKNN collaborative filtering¶

ItemKNN uses cosine similarity between channel columns of the sparse user-channel matrix. A candidate channel receives a high score if it is similar to channels already watched by the viewer.

The implementation avoids materializing a dense 162k × 162k similarity matrix. Instead it multiplies sparse blocks and retains only each item's top-k neighbors.

Selected setting: k = 200.

In [7]:
# Simplified from src/twitch_recs/models/item_knn.py
# Actual repository code uses sparse block multiplication and keeps only top-k similarities.

def itemknn_score(user_row, sparse_similarity):
    """Score every candidate item from the user's watched-item row."""
    return (user_row @ sparse_similarity).toarray().ravel()

3.3 Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR)¶

BPR learns latent user and channel vectors from pairwise comparisons. For a user (u), observed channel (i), and sampled unobserved channel (j), training pushes:

[ s(u,i) > s(u,j) ]

This is better aligned with implicit ranking than ordinary least squares or treating every missing interaction as a negative class.

Selected BPR settings: 64 factors, learning rate 0.03, regularization 0.0025, 20 epochs, 1 sampled negative per positive, seed 258.

In [8]:
# Simplified from src/twitch_recs/models/bpr.py
def bpr_pairwise_step(user_vec, pos_vec, neg_vec, learning_rate, regularization):
    diff = user_vec @ (pos_vec - neg_vec)
    gradient = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(diff))  # sigmoid(-diff)
    # The repository updates user, positive-item, and negative-item factors
    # using this pairwise gradient plus L2 regularization.
    return gradient

3.4 Hybrid model¶

ItemKNN and BPR scores have different scales. For each user, the repository standardizes each model's eligible candidate scores, then combines them:

[ \text{Hybrid}(u,i) = \alpha , z(\text{BPR}_{u,i})

  • (1-\alpha), z(\text{ItemKNN}_{u,i}) -\lambda \log(1+\text{popularity}_i) ]

Validation selected α = 0.75 and λ = 0.

A non-zero popularity penalty increased novelty / long-tail exposure, but did not improve validation NDCG@10, so it was not selected for the final model.

3.5 Feature representation ablation¶

We also test a count-weighted ItemKNN that preserves repeated interaction counts instead of binarizing them. This directly tests whether repeated consumption should dominate similarity for the new-channel discovery objective.

The held-out results show that binary ItemKNN is better for ranking accuracy, while count-weighting increases coverage and novelty. This is a useful example of a feature representation changing the optimization tradeoff rather than simply being "better" or "worse".

4.3 Statistical validity — user-clustered paired bootstrap¶

There are 104,818 user/day cases but only 53,212 eligible users, so multiple rows from the same user are correlated. Treating every case as independent would overstate certainty.

We therefore resample users (not individual user/day rows) with replacement 10,000 times, retaining all cases for each sampled user. Holm correction controls family-wise error across the reported comparisons.

4.10 Matched-protocol scalability and robustness¶

The updated scalability experiment makes the scale comparison substantially more controlled than the earlier version.

We now have four completed cohort-scale runs — 100k, 200k, 500k, and 1m users — with the following protocol held constant:

  • random seed = 258
  • the same hyperparameter search grid
  • at most 2,000 validation users
  • at most 5,000 test users
  • exact ranking over the full fitted candidate catalog
  • the same chronological train / validation / test definition
  • the same new-channel target construction and ranking metrics

This removes the previous protocol mismatch where the small run evaluated essentially all eligible users while the larger runs used capped samples. The experiment is therefore useful evidence about engineering scalability and robustness of the model conclusions across cohort sizes.

What this experiment does and does not prove¶

This is now a matched-protocol scalability and robustness experiment, and it supports two useful claims:

  1. the disk-backed pipeline can complete training, tuning, refit, and exact full-catalog ranking through 1 million users under the same evaluation design;
  2. the main model conclusion — Hybrid > ItemKNN > BPR > popularity — is robust across the four cohort sizes.

However, it is still not a causal learning curve. The 100k, 200k, 500k, and 1m cohorts are not nested, the candidate catalogs expand with cohort size, and the eligible sampled users / future targets are not identical across runs. Therefore we should not claim that changing training size alone caused any model's score to rise or fall.

A true training-size experiment would keep the test users, target items, candidate catalog, evaluation protocol, and tuning budget fixed while adding nested training data.

5. Related Work¶

5.1 LiveRec and the source dataset¶

Rappaz, McAuley, and Aberer (RecSys 2021), “Recommendation on Live-Streaming Platforms: Dynamic Availability and Repeat Consumption,” introduced the Twitch dataset and LiveRec.

Their key observation is that live-stream recommendation differs from conventional static-item recommendation because:

  • availability changes over time,
  • not watching a stream does not necessarily mean disliking it,
  • repeat consumption of the same streamer is central.

Our project deliberately studies a different target: discovery of channels the viewer has never watched before. Therefore, our numerical results should not be treated as a replication of LiveRec.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1145/3460231.3474267

5.2 Bayesian Personalized Ranking¶

Rendle et al. (2009), “BPR: Bayesian Personalized Ranking from Implicit Feedback,” motivates pairwise ranking for implicit data rather than treating all missing entries as ordinary negative labels.

That formulation matches the structure of this project, although our result shows that a simple item-item co-view model is much stronger than our BPR implementation on this particular new-channel protocol.

Source: https://proceedings.mlr.press/r7/rendle09a.html

5.3 LightGCN and graph collaborative filtering¶

He et al. (2020), “LightGCN: Simplifying and Powering Graph Convolution Network for Recommendation,” simplifies graph collaborative filtering to neighborhood aggregation on the user-item graph.

We include LightGCN as an additional experiment, but it does not outperform ItemKNN or the Hybrid under our current protocol. This is a useful negative result: a more complex model is not automatically better when candidate availability, sparsity, and objective alignment dominate.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1145/3397271.3401063

6. Conclusions¶

What worked¶

  • Personalized models substantially outperform popularity baselines.
  • ItemKNN is the strongest individual primary model.
  • Hybrid produces the best held-out ranking accuracy.
  • The Hybrid's improvement over ItemKNN is statistically significant under user-clustered paired bootstrap.
  • Binary watch history is better aligned with new-channel discovery than count-weighted repeat interaction.
  • Model ordering is stable under period-level evaluation, rolling-origin windows, BPR seeds, and validation-user samples.
  • The same ordering is also stable across matched-protocol 100k / 200k / 500k / 1m scale runs.

What did not work as well¶

  • BPR is much weaker than ItemKNN alone.
  • Advanced sequential / graph models do not beat the simpler primary models in the current offline setup.
  • Long-tail targets and long-history users remain difficult.
  • Exact live availability is unavailable, so the active-7d experiment is only a proxy.
  • Roughly 12% of novel test positives are catalog-cold and cannot be ranked by these collaborative models.

Final answer to the research question¶

Yes — historical viewing behavior is strongly predictive of future new-channel discovery, and combining complementary ItemKNN and BPR signals improves Top-10 ranking accuracy.
However, the best accuracy comes with a measurable diversity cost, and the system remains strongly biased toward popular, previously observed channels.
